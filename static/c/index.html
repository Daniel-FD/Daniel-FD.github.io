Compliance, at its simplest, is bookkeeping on a transaction graph. Ignoring fees, mints and burns, the balance bi held at address i changes only by what flows in minus what flows out,

dbi/dt = Σj fj→i − Σj fi→j,(1)

where fj→i is the rate at which value moves from j to i. Much of the job is tracing the terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (1) back to where they came from.

3Principles

I state the principles I work by as propositions. Each one names what it costs, so each one could turn out to be wrong.2None of these has been proved. All of them have been tested, some of them the hard way. Section 4 argues for them at more length.

Proposition 3.1 (Problem before model). I name who is hurting, how often and what it costs before I pick a method; it slows the first week and saves the next six months.

Proposition 3.2 (Show, don't tell). A working prototype in front of the person who will use it settles more than any slide; I would rather be corrected in week two than applauded in month six.

Proposition 3.3 (Own the whole path). From raw data to the interface and the conversation with the customer, because most failures live in the hand-offs, and the fewer hand-offs there are, the fewer places a problem can hide.

Proposition 3.4 (Simplest thing first). Rules, then classic machine learning, then an LLM; I climb a rung only when the one below has failed on a test set, because each rung buys flexibility with predictability, cost and explainability.

Proposition 3.5 (Evaluation is the product). If it can't be measured on cases people agree on, nobody knows whether it works; so the labelled set, and the cost of each kind of error, come before the model.

Proposition 3.6 (AI amplifies judgement; it doesn't replace it). A model makes a good decision faster and a bad one faster too, so taste and responsibility stay with a person, and I design the hand-over to that person as carefully as the model.

Proposition 3.7 (Uncertainty is information). When the evidence is unclear, the answer is “review”, not “pass”; a system that admits doubt is safer than one that hides it.

Proposition 3.8 (Claim only what is true). I say what was deliberately left out and label demo data as fictional; scope said out loud beats a surprise later.

Proposition 3.9 (Status before anyone asks). I work async and in writing: decisions and their reasons live where the next person will look, and risks are flagged early, so nobody has to chase me.

Proposition 3.10 (Change my mind with data). I teach what I learn, intuition first and maths second, because explaining is the quickest test of understanding; when the data disagree with me, the data win.

4Discussion

4.1On product: from the problem to the person using it

Most models I've seen fail didn't fail on accuracy. They failed somewhere between the notebook and the person who was meant to use them. So I start at the other end. Who is this for? What do they do today, and what does it cost them when it goes wrong? I want one concrete example before any abstraction, and the root cause rather than the symptom.

Then I build the smallest thing that works and put it in front of a domain expert, early. Their feedback is rarely about the model. It is about what to fix first, and whether a number comes with its reason. Sentences like these reshape a product more than any hyperparameter. (The hyperparameters are usually fine.)

The model is rarely the product. The decision it changes is. At Citizens Advice Scotland, where I led the AI system (§1.2), the result that mattered was a caller reaching their local bureau in under a minute instead of waiting up to 24 hours for a callback. I try to describe all my work that way: what a person can now do that they couldn't before, and what we chose not to build.

4.2On AI and judgement

I use LLMs every day, and I trust them the way I'd trust a bright new colleague: a lot, once I've checked their work. The checking is the job. Before I change a prompt, I want a set of real cases, labelled by people who agree on the answers, and a clear view of which mistakes are expensive. In compliance, a missed risky flow costs far more than one extra alert. In advice services, a wrong referral costs someone time they may not have. Those costs set the threshold, not the model.

Then I climb the ladder slowly. A rule, if a rule will do. A classic model, if the rule can't. An LLM when the input is messy language: summarising long text, drafting a first reply, pulling structure out of free-form notes. That is where they earn their keep. An LLM is a superb first draft and a poor last word.

The aim is not to automate judgement. It is to give the person who carries the responsibility a better starting point, and more time to think.

4.3On working remotely

In a remote team, writing is the office. The document is the meeting room, the pull request is the whiteboard, and the channel is the corridor. So I write for the reader who wasn't there: the conclusion first, then the evidence, then the one point I'd most like them to push on.

Two habits do most of the work. The first is status before anyone asks: what moved, what's stuck, and the risk I can see coming, sent before someone has to chase it. Bad news travels best early and in writing. The second is decisions in the open. When we choose A over B, the reasons go where the next person will look, so that a colleague six months from now can work out why without finding me. Large changes arrive as a short series of small ones, in an order a reviewer can hold in their head.

Demos beat meetings. Ten minutes of the real thing running answers questions that an hour of slides would only raise. None of this is about working alone. It is how a team that rarely shares a room still thinks together.

4.4On the thread through my work

I didn't plan a theme, but looking back there is one. My PhD asked how to clear smoke from homes that burn unclean fuels indoors, where a fan is not an option [P1]. I studied it in a lab tank, with salty water standing in for the smoke [P2]. The most useful finding was almost disappointingly simple: line the heat source up with the opening.

Then the advice sector, helping people reach a human adviser faster. Then medical imaging, diagnosing and measuring from images. Now financial safety: tracing where money has been, so that it is harder to move it where it shouldn't go.

The domains look unrelated. The method is not: measure carefully, fit the simplest model that explains the data, and say plainly what it can't explain. Nor are the people. In each case, someone at the other end of the system had less time, money or information than the system did. That seems to me a good reason to get the details right.
